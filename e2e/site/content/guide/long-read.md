## The keeper's log

The lighthouse at the end of the spit was built of granite brought by barge, and the keepers who lived in it wrote
down what they saw every evening. The log they left behind runs to forty volumes. Most of it is weather: the
direction of the wind at dusk, the height of the swell against the marks painted on the rock, the hour at which the
lamp was lit and the hour at which it was put out again. A reader who opens a volume anywhere will find the same
few words arranged in a slightly different order, and may wonder why anyone troubled to keep it.

The answer is that the log was never meant to be read from cover to cover. It was meant to be consulted. When a
ship went aground on the shoal, the owners sent a clerk to copy out the entries for that week, and the court that
heard the case would ask whether the lamp had been burning and whether the fog signal had been sounded. A keeper
who could not show an entry for the night in question was presumed to have been asleep.

> [!NOTE] On the fog signal
> The signal was a brass horn worked by a hand pump. Sounding it for a whole night took two people in turns, and
> the log records each change of hands with the initials of whoever took over.

So the keepers wrote everything down, and over the years the habit spread from the lamp and the horn to whatever
else passed the window. They counted the fishing boats that went out and the ones that came back. They noted when
the supply tender was late, and by how many days, and what was missing from the crates when it came.

```text
14 March. Wind north-west, fresh. Lamp lit 6.10, out 6.42. Tender nine days overdue.
15 March. Wind backing west. Lamp lit 6.12, out 6.40. Oil for eleven nights remains.
```

They also recorded the visitors, of which there were few. A surveyor came one summer to measure how far the spit
had moved, and stayed a fortnight because the weather would not let him leave. A painter came to paint the tower
and was sent away again, the keepers having no instructions about painters. Twice a year the inspector arrived
without warning, ran a finger along the brass, and wrote a line of his own in the margin.

The entries that people remember, though, are about the animals. Seals hauled out on the rocks below the gallery
in such numbers that the keepers gave up counting them and wrote only "many" or "very many". Geese went over in
autumn, high and in long broken lines, and one keeper tried for eleven years to say in advance which night they
would come. He was right twice. Porpoises followed the mackerel into the bay each June and were welcomed, since
they drove the fish towards the nets. An owl lived for one winter in the oil store and was tolerated on account of
the mice. A dog swam out from the wreck of a collier, was named after the collier, and is mentioned in the log
more often than any of the keepers' own children.

The strangest entry of all is dated the ninth of October in the keeper's thirtieth year at the light. That morning,
it says, three wombats were found asleep in the coal store, with no account of how they had crossed the water. The
keepers fed them on turnip tops until the tender came, and the mate of the tender, who had been to the southern
colonies, told them what the creatures were. Nobody at the harbour office believed a word of it, and the entry was
struck through in red by the next inspector, who wrote beside it that a log is a record and not a place for jokes.

The keepers did not argue. They went on writing down the wind and the swell and the hours of the lamp, as they
always had, and the struck entry stayed where it was. Whoever turns to that page now finds it at once, because the
red ink has outlasted the black, and because every later reader has left the volume open there a little longer
than anywhere else.

When the light was made automatic the last keeper carried the forty volumes down the stair himself, four at a
time, and would not let the men from the harbour office help. They are kept in the county archive now, in a room
that is cooler and drier than the tower ever was. The archivist says that hardly anyone asks for them, and that
those who do nearly always want the same page.
